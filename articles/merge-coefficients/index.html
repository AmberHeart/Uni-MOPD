Are merge coefficients worth tuning before distillation?

Hand-tuned and calibrated merges move the score before MOPD by −2.0 to +1.1 points; after 50 updates the best of them are level with the uniform merge. Lower calibration loss does not pick a better start. The total scale is what can break a merge, and MOPD repairs it.

Not if you distill afterwards. Searching or fitting the merge coefficients moves the score before MOPD by −2.0 to +1.1 points, but after 50 MOPD updates the best searched merges are level with the uniform one. Uniform weights are not the optimal merge and need not be; what matters is the total scale.

The uniform merge matches calibrated starts

We compared the uniform merge (every coefficient 0.2) with two kinds of search. The tuned merge is the best of a manual search that up-weights the two strongest specialists, OCR and GUI, and checks every candidate on all 19 benchmarks, which makes it costly and a deliberately strong reference. Calibrated merges fit the five coefficients offline to the teachers, without using benchmark scores.

Short MOPD narrows the differences: across the nine merges we continued, a 3.1-point spread before MOPD shrinks to 1.1 after 50 updates. Merges that start ahead give back their lead, and weaker ones catch up; PGD@5 and PGD@8 reach 69.5 and 69.7 at update 200, against 69.4 for the uniform run.

Lower calibration loss does not pick a better start

Along the PGD path the calibration loss falls from 0.73 to 0.44, yet the score before MOPD does not improve and drops to 65.6 at PGD@5. The loss is measured on responses the teachers wrote, whereas the benchmarks score responses the merged model writes itself. The coefficients show what the loss rewards.

Scope.

PGD calibration from three initial strengths; only the strength-1 path was continued with MOPD.

From every initial strength, the fit pushed the STEM and VQA coefficients toward zero and grew the other three, ending at about the same loss. A likely reason is that lowering the average loss on teacher trajectories is served by giving up two domains to serve three, and nothing in the loss sees what this does to generation. The paper’s four-objective study points the same way: the reverse-KL fit drives the VQA coefficient to 0.05 and costs 0.7 points, while cross-entropy and forward KL, which gain 1.1, stay close to the tuned merge.

What the calibrated merges give up

The coefficients also show which benchmarks pay. Before MOPD, PGD@8 loses 10.5 points on MMMU-Pro against the uniform merge, and PGD@5 loses 18.2 on MathVision and 11.5 on MMMU-Pro. The STEM specialist leads on both benchmarks (64.5 and 68.4), and the fit shrank its coefficient. Both merges also lose about 10 points on CharXiv (9.7 and 10.2) and gain 2.8 to 4.3 on ScreenSpot-Pro and OSWorld-G, where the GUI and OCR specialists lead and the fit grew their coefficients.

Short MOPD repairs the STEM side: after 50 updates PGD@8 is 1.2 below the uniform merge on MMMU-Pro and 0.8 above on MathVision. The remaining deficits lie on the other shrunken coefficient. PGD@8 and PGD@5 still trail on MUIRBench by 4.2 and 3.9 and on TreeBench by 4.9 and 4.4, the two benchmarks where the VQA specialist leads (74.7 and 48.6), and both keep their lead on ScreenSpot-Pro (3.0 and 2.5). One reading is that the shrunken VQA coefficient leaves less of the VQA specialist in these starts, and 50 updates restore the STEM side but not yet this one.

The total scale can break a merge

The shape of the coefficients barely matters after distillation, but their sum can break the merge outright.

  • The score peaks between scale 1 and 1.25. On a 13-benchmark subset, 1.25 is slightly ahead of scale 1 (61.7 vs. 61.3), and 0.75 is 1.9 points lower.
  • At scale 2 the 19-benchmark average falls from 67.6 to 61.2, and the mean response grows from 787 to 6,587 tokens.
  • At scale 3 answers run to tens of thousands of tokens; in our evaluations MathVision, CharXiv and ScreenSpot-v2 fall to 4.7, 0.8 and 11.1.

MOPD repairs the collapse, slowly. The scale-3 merge is at 66.4 after 50 updates and 68.9 after 150, back among the strong starts but still 0.6 below the main run at update 200. During training we observed the rollouts shorten within the first few dozen updates, most in GUI. An overscaled merge thus costs updates and part of the final score; a scale near one costs neither.

DARE versus plain averaging

DARE drops a random fraction λ of each task vector and rescales the rest by 1/(1−λ); with λ=0 the merge is plain averaging. At scale 1, every drop rate we tried scores at least as high as averaging (panel b of the first scale figure above).

Even dropping 90% of every task vector does not hurt. Our reading is that the specialists’ updates are largely redundant. The main comparisons use λ=0.7.

In practice

  • Use uniform weights at total scale one, and skip the coefficient search if you are going to distill.
  • Don’t choose coefficients by a calibration loss on teacher outputs.
  • If a merge produces runaway responses, check the coefficient sum first.
